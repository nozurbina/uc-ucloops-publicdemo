# Journey Stages — Omar

## Stage 1: The Trusted Tip

### Goals:
- Register a credible new lunch option without spending any real decision effort on it — a trusted colleague de-risks the first try
- Confirm the truck sits within a viable walk of my desk and my calendar
- Keep lunch a solved problem — I'm not shopping, I'm filing

### Narrative:
We're walking back from a meeting near King and Priyesh points at a truck and says "the brisket one." That's the entire pitch, and honestly, that's the right length. I don't follow food accounts, I don't read reviews — I don't have the minutes, and lunch is a problem I solved years ago. But a recommendation from a guy who bills by the hour carries weight. I clock the corner: maybe four minutes from the lobby. Fine. It goes in the mental file under "try when the calendar can absorb a line." Anyway. Filed.

### Questions:
- Is this actually good, or is it just new to Priyesh?
- How far is that corner from my desk, door to door?
- Is it there every weekday, or does it move around?
- What does the line look like at peak — 12:15 to 12:45?

### Problems:
- I have zero appetite for evaluating a new lunch option — every minute spent researching food is a minute the client doesn't get back
- I can see the truck but I can't see the commitment: no idea if it's a six-minute stop or a twenty-five-minute gamble
- A recommendation is not a schedule — "it's usually there" is doing a lot of work in that sentence
- My calendar this week has no slot that can absorb an unknown line

### Tasks:
- Take the colleague's recommendation on board
- Clock the truck's location relative to the office
- File it mentally for a low-stakes day
- Scan the calendar for a lunch window with slack in it

### Sentiment:
Guarded interest

### Think/Feel Quote:
"Fine — one lunch slot. That's the entire budget for this experiment."

### Alternate Paths:
- The colleague's tip never lines up with a free window and the idea quietly expires
- I Google "lunch near me" instead and the truck doesn't surface, so the default sandwich place wins again
- I walk past on a busy day, see fifteen people in line, and file it under "not for me"

### Channels:
- Colleague word of mouth
- Walking past the truck
- Google "lunch near me"
- Outlook calendar (checking the window)

## Stage 2: The Solo Vet

### Goals:
- Verify the quality-to-time ratio in one visit: very good brisket in six minutes, not the best brisket in the city
- Understand the failure modes I'd be signing up for — line length, and whether the truck is even there
- Time the full desk-to-desk loop against a real between-meetings window

### Narrative:
Thursday, a rare 90-minute gap, so I go. Reluctantly — a line at 12:40 is a real risk to a 1 o'clock, and I have been the guy who walks in late holding a taco. Once. Not again. Eight people ahead of me; I time it. Eleven minutes to the window, order the smoked brisket taco, and — it's genuinely good. Actually smoked, not a sauce pretending. Good enough that I registered it, which food rarely does when I'm in work mode. Desk to desk: twenty-two minutes. Eighty percent of a restaurant for twenty percent of the time cost. That ratio, I'll take.

### Questions:
- Does the line move at a predictable rate, or does one complicated order stall everything?
- Is the brisket like this every time, or did I catch a good day?
- Do they sell out — and when?
- What's my worst-case desk-to-desk time if I hit peak?

### Problems:
- The queue is an unpriced cost — eight people could be nine minutes or nineteen, and I can't tell from the back of the line
- No visibility on sellouts: I half-heard the guy two ahead of me get told the brisket was low, and I did the maths on having queued for nothing
- One data point is not reliability — a single great taco tells me about the food, not about the operation
- Standing in a line is the one part of this that doesn't scale with my calendar

### Tasks:
- Walk down on a low-stakes day and queue once
- Order the smoked brisket taco
- Time the full desk-to-desk loop
- Judge whether the loop fits a standard 45-minute window, line included

### Sentiment:
Impressed, calculating

### Think/Feel Quote:
"Very good brisket in eleven minutes of queue. The taco passed; now the operation has to."

### Alternate Paths:
- The line is fifteen deep and I bail without ordering — experiment postponed, not failed
- Brisket sold out at the window; I take the carne asada, which is fine, but "fine" doesn't change a routine
- The truck isn't there at all and the whole idea takes a credibility hit on day one

### Channels:
- Walking past the truck
- The truck window
- The line itself (watching throughput)
- Phone clock/timer

## Stage 3: Into the Rotation

### Goals:
- Solve lunch once and never re-solve it — make BorderBlend the default known quantity
- Turn the grudging solo-run-for-four into a clean group order: four labelled items, one bag, one pickup
- Answer the pre-departure question every time: open, where, how long is the line

### Narrative:
I didn't decide to become a regular. It happened. Two, three times a week now, and increasingly it's "I'll grab lunch, what does everyone want" — so I'm carrying four orders in my head, tracking who's plant-based and who skips the salsa verde. The food has earned its slot; my job is protecting the slot. So I downloaded the app to order ahead — tap from the desk, walk down, one bag waiting. That was the dream. The app didn't deliver it. Couldn't tell if the truck was actually open, menu didn't match the board, no "ready" moment. Used it twice. Closed it. Anyway. Back to walking up and taking my chances — a failure mode I at least understand.

### Questions:
- Before I leave the desk: is it open today, where exactly, and how long is the line right now?
- Can I place one order with four labelled items instead of reciting four orders at the window?
- Why does the app menu not match the board — which one is lying?
- Will an order-ahead actually be bagged when I arrive, or am I standing there anyway?

### Problems:
- The worst outcome keeps being possible: ten minutes walked, empty corner, 1 o'clock looming, and now I'm improvising — the exact thing I was trying to avoid
- The app added uncertainty instead of removing it — I half-decided on something at my desk that wasn't even on the board when I got there
- No clear "your order is ready" moment, so order-ahead just relocates the waiting instead of deleting it
- Carrying four colleagues' orders verbally through a truck window is an error-prone process I would never accept in my actual work
- Twenty minutes of line is the difference between early for my next thing and the guy who walks in late holding a taco

### Tasks:
- Fold the truck into the weekly rotation
- Collect and carry colleagues' orders, dietary quirks included
- Trial the app's order-ahead workflow; abandon it when it underperforms walking up
- Recite, verify, and distribute four orders per group run
- Expense each run off a crumpled thermal receipt

### Sentiment:
Committed, mildly exasperated

### Think/Feel Quote:
"The food's a solved problem. The getting-to-the-food is the part still costing me minutes."

### Alternate Paths:
- One empty-corner strike too many and the rotation slot quietly goes back to the boring-but-certain food court
- A colleague's order comes back wrong (salsa verde on the no-salsa one) and I stop volunteering for group runs
- I keep the habit solo-only and the group business — four covers a run, several runs a week — never materializes for them

### Channels:
- BorderBlend app
- The truck window
- Walking past the truck
- Slack with colleagues ("I'll grab lunch, what does everyone want")
- Expense system (receipt upload)

## Stage 4: Proving the Operation

### Goals:
- Expense cleanly, forty times a quarter: a proper itemized invoice, emailed, with a cost centre or client code
- Find the front door for a defined, paid catering event — the working lunch that beats the same three sad sandwich platters
- Put my name on a team order with confidence — I vouch for things I've verified, and I've verified the food

### Narrative:
The stakes went up quietly. It's not just my lunch now — I've vouched for this truck to my team, and I've been asked to sort food for a working session: twelve people, client in the room, nobody breaking for an hour. I'd genuinely rather roll up a taco spread than platter number three. So I try to book it and — there's no front door. No obvious way to say "I would like to give you a defined amount of money for a defined event." I'm the customer waving money. Meanwhile finance has flagged my fading thermal receipts twice this quarter. I'm not asking them to convince me. I'm already convinced. Make it easy to say yes.

### Questions:
- Who do I actually ask about catering — the guy at the window, a form, an email address that a human reads?
- Can I get an itemized invoice, emailed as a PDF, with a client code on it?
- Can they commit to a headcount and a time in writing — something I can forward internally?
- What happens to my twelve-person order if the truck has a normal lunchtime rush at the same time?

### Problems:
- There is no discoverable path to book catering — I'm trying to hand over a large, defined amount of money and I can't find the door
- A crumpled thermal receipt that's already fading is not what my finance team wants to see, forty times a quarter
- Half these lunches are billable to a project, and without a cost centre or client code on paper the friction lands on me at month-end
- Asking at the window mid-rush gets me a nod and a verbal "yeah, probably" — which is not a thing I can put in front of a client session
- If I vouch for this and the order shows up short or late in front of a client, that's my name on it, not theirs

### Tasks:
- Ask at the window who handles group and catering orders
- Request the itemized emailed invoice with cost-centre field
- Scope the twelve-person working-lunch order: headcount, dietary needs, timing
- Chase down a written confirmation solid enough to forward internally
- Run the session order and debrief myself on whether it landed on time, at count

### Sentiment:
Convinced, impatient

### Think/Feel Quote:
"I'm the easiest sale they'll get all year. Someone just has to pick up the money."

### Alternate Paths:
- No bookable path materializes in time and the sad sandwich platters win by default — their loss, literally
- The invoice request gets a shrug, so I keep the solo habit but route group and client work to a vendor with paperwork
- The order lands perfectly, on time, at count — and I go from customer to reference: "use the brisket truck, tell them I sent you"

### Channels:
- The truck window (asking who to talk to)
- borderblend.ca (hunting for a catering page)
- E-mail (invoice and booking requests)
- Slack with colleagues (collecting the twelve orders)
- Expense/finance system

## Stage 5: Opting In

### Goals:
- Never walk down to an empty corner again — plan the midday around one trustworthy notification
- Graduate BorderBlend from "the truck I take my chances on" to load-bearing infrastructure
- Keep converting alerts into group runs — two to four colleagues, several times a week, happily

### Narrative:
They shipped the thing I'd have specced myself: a lunch alert. Push at 11:30 — "Bay Street truck open till 2, brisket in stock." I'm a man whose inbox bar is very high; I unsubscribe from things for sport. I signed up for this one on the spot, because it's not marketing, it's operational information. One notification and my entire midday is planned. And the day it said "not out today — back tomorrow," that was the moment I actually trusted it. Tell me the bad news early and I believe the good news. So: alerts on, loyalty account created, group runs booked off the morning push. It's not my favourite restaurant. It's better than that. It's infrastructure. You don't churn out of infrastructure.

### Questions:
- Does the alert stay accurate — open, located, stocked exactly as stated?
- Will this list stay pure signal, or does the brand-story content start creeping in?
- Does the loyalty side ever translate into something operational — like priority pickup on group runs?
- If the truck moves spots or seasons change, does the alert move with it?

### Problems:
- One false alert — "brisket in stock" and it isn't — re-opens every bit of uncertainty this subscription exists to close
- If the feed drifts into recipes and meet-the-team content, I'm gone in four seconds, and taking the useful signal down with it
- The alert solves me, but the group order still lives in my head and at the window — the last un-automated step in the routine
- A loyalty programme framed as points and badges is noise; I don't need to be rewarded, I need to be informed

### Tasks:
- Subscribe to the lunch alert and allow the morning push
- Act on the first alert and verify it against reality
- Trust the honest bad-news alert and re-plan at my desk instead of on a cold corner
- Convert the morning push into group runs — take orders in Slack, walk down once
- Recommend the alert to the colleagues I keep feeding

### Sentiment:
Settled, quietly loyal

### Think/Feel Quote:
"One accurate notification a day. That's the whole product. That's the whole relationship."

### Alternate Paths:
- An alert misfires once; I keep the subscription but reinstate the walk-up-and-check habit until it re-earns the trust
- The list turns into a newsletter with a brand story; unsubscribe, keep the lunch habit, lose the channel
- The alerts prove reliable for a full quarter and I move the team's monthly working lunch onto them by default — routine, not enthusiasm, and worth more

### Channels:
- BorderBlend app (push notifications, loyalty sign-up)
- E-mail (alert list confirmation)
- Slack with colleagues (converting alerts into group runs)
- The truck window
- Walking past the truck (verification, old habits)
