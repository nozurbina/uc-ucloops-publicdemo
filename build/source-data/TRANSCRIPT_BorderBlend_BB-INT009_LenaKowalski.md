Participant recruited through consumer panel. Runs a Vancouver food blog (approximately 8,000 monthly readers). Has reviewed BorderBlend twice — once positively, once with mixed observations. ## Transcript

**Interviewer:** You've reviewed BorderBlend twice. What's the arc of your impressions?

**Lena:** The first review was on the strength of the product. The smoked brisket taco is genuinely excellent — I stand by that. The technique is real. The flavour combinations are thoughtful. The fusion identity is honest rather than pretentious. My first review reflected that. Strong positive.

The second review was prompted by a visit where something was off. The brisket was good, as always. But the service was slower than I'd experienced, the truck was understaffed, and a limited-edition item I'd specifically gone for was sold out by noon even though the promotion suggested it would be available all day. My second review reflected that — acknowledged the food quality, noted the execution gaps.

**Interviewer:** How did the brand respond to the second review?

**Lena:** They didn't, to my public review. I did get a DM from the account, politely, saying they appreciated the feedback and that the limited-edition availability issue was a supply problem. That was fine. Honest. I appreciated the transparency. But I thought they could have engaged more publicly — responded to the review with the same explanation. Transparency on the issue in a public channel would have been a stronger signal than a private DM.

**Interviewer:** What do you think makes BorderBlend's brand positioning work and where does it fall short?

**Lena:** What works: the specificity. "Smoked brisket, pickled red onion, salsa verde. On a flour tortilla." That is genuinely how the brand talks about its food and it's right. It doesn't oversell. It doesn't use ten adjectives. It lets the specific combination carry the argument.

What falls short: the environmental and sourcing story. For a brand that is explicit about its fusion identity — "we're Mexican-Canadian with Korean influence, we're honest about what we are" — there's no equivalent transparency about where the ingredients come from. The brisket in particular. My readers ask about that. If I can't answer "where is the beef from," that's a gap the brand should care about.

**Interviewer:** Is that a dealbreaker for your readers?

**Lena:** For some of them, yes. Vancouver food culture skews sustainability-conscious. A significant portion of my readership won't visit a food business without some kind of sourcing story. BorderBlend has nothing to say publicly about this. It might be a deliberate decision — it's harder to source sustainably at food-truck economics — but saying nothing is worse than addressing it honestly.

**Interviewer:** What about the brand's social media?

**Lena:** Inconsistent. The national account has good content — when they do the food-specific close-up photography it's excellent. But the posting frequency isn't reliable, and when they go quiet for weeks it looks like the brand is struggling, even if it isn't. The local Vancouver account is even more inconsistent. The truck I frequent most often doesn't post at all, as far as I can tell. I've told people to check if the truck is running and they've found no recent posts — some people gave up and didn't go.

**Interviewer:** Have you seen any brand content that particularly impressed you?

**Lena:** There was a post explaining the origin of the Korean-Mexican combination — how it developed in Los Angeles from Korean-owned taco trucks that started serving in Latino neighbourhoods. That's a real story, historically accurate, and it gives the fusion identity context rather than just asserting it. I reposted that on my blog. That's the content I can endorse as accurate and worth sharing.

**Interviewer:** What about content that felt off to you?

**Lena:** Superlatives. Any food brand that calls its product "the best" anything has lost me. BorderBlend generally avoids this but there have been moments — "the best tacos in [city]" in a story, probably informal, probably intended as playful. Even playful superlatives undermine credibility for me. The brand has earned my trust by not overclaiming. Moments of overclaiming chip at that.

**Interviewer:** What would make you a more engaged advocate for the brand?

**Lena:** Sourcing transparency. A genuine story about where the beef comes from. If it's not sustainable, say what the plan is. If it is sustainable, say so clearly and specifically — not vague "responsibly sourced" language. That's the single thing that would move the brand from "good food" in my writing to "good food with a good story."

**Interviewer:** Any final thoughts?

**Lena:** The food has enough quality to anchor a much stronger brand story. The fusion identity is genuinely interesting. The sourcing question is the gap between being a good food truck and being a brand people follow because they believe in it. That gap can be closed.

**Interviewer:** Really useful perspective. Thank you.
